Hello, dear friends. In this lesson, we are starting a brand new section. Yes, in the very first lesson of this new section, we'll talk about the architecture of Databricks. That is, how its infrastructure works. When we say architecture, what we really mean is this. What components are behind the system, where are they located, and how do they communicate with each other? You can think of it like the layout of a city. In a city, roads, buildings, power lines, and water systems all work together in harmony. Databricks also has such an infrastructure plan. Friends, the architecture of Databricks consists of two main parts, the control plane and the compute plane. The control plane is like the brain of Databricks. The user interface you see when logging in through your browser, cluster management, security and access controls such as Unity Catalog, all of these run here. Small but important details like previous versions of your notebooks or job history are also stored here. In other words, you can think of the control plane as the system's management center. On the other side, we have the compute plane. This is the muscle of Databricks. Actual data processing, Spark jobs, and SQL queries all take place here. For example, suppose you have a large Excel table. When you want to perform filtering or aggregation, the compute plane does that work. Now we can examine the compute plane under two categories, classic compute and serverless compute. So what is classic compute? In this model, the machines are launched inside your own cloud account. If you're using Azure, the machines run in your Azure subscription within a dedicated resource group. If you're on AWS, the machines are launched in your AWS account as EC2 instances. If you're on GCP, the machines live inside your Google project. You can think of this like owning your own car. The car's in your garage, you drive it, and all costs are your responsibility. Serverless compute, on the other hand, works differently. Here, the machines are not in your account. They are hosted in Databricks' own subscription. When you request a cluster, Databricks quickly assigns you a machine from this pool. This is like taking a taxi. The vehicle is ready, you call it, use it for your job, and then it goes back. You don't deal with maintenance or setup. The main advantage is that it starts up very quickly. When working in Databricks, you decide whether to use classic or serverless compute. When creating a new cluster or SQL warehouse, you specify which compute type you want. If you choose classic compute, the machines are launched in your cloud subscription. You have control. You can choose the machine size configuration and make custom adjustments. If you choose serverless compute, the machines are already waiting in Databricks' subscription. This model starts much faster, and you don't deal with infrastructure management. It's especially ideal for short tasks and ad hoc queries. For those using community edition, there is no such choice. Databricks automatically provides a serverless-like environment in the background, and you can start using it directly. Now, let's move on to the storage part. When you create a workspace in Databricks, a storage area is automatically set up as well. This is where system data such as notebook histories, job records, and Spark logs are stored. You can also save your temporary data here. You can think of storage as the cities archive room or warehouse. All documents, records, and data are kept here in an organized way. In summary, control plane is the management center, like the brain. Compute plane is the muscle, the factory that does the work. Classic equals your own car, runs in your cloud account. Serverless equals a taxi. Machines are ready in Databricks' count. Storage is the archive room where data is stored. Yes, I explained the topic using metaphors. I'll end the lesson here.
