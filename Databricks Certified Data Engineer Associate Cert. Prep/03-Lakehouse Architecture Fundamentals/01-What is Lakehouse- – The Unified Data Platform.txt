Lecture: What is Lakehouse? – The Unified Data Platform
----------------------------------------

Dear friends, hello everyone. In this lesson, we will greet a new section. And in the very first class of this section, we will meet one of the most talked about concepts in the data world, the lakehouse. Friends, today we will learn about lakehouse architecture, one of the most important modern data approaches. To understand this concept, we first need to look back at the data management methods used in the past, along with their strengths and limitation, because the lakehouse emerged as a solution that combines these methods. It's not a standalone option that just appeared out of nowhere. That's why we'll proceed chronologically. Friends, it all started with a data warehouse. Since the 1980s, companies have been storing their data in data warehouses. These systems were especially suitable for structured data, that is, data organized into tables, rows, and columns. The advantage was clear. The data was very well organized, reports ran fast, and business analysts could easily access the information they needed to make decisions. To make it more concrete, think of a data warehouse as an official archive building. Documents are placed in folders, stored neatly on shelves, everything is highly organized. But this archive only accepts standard file formats. If a document comes in a different format, it won't be stored inside. Of course, as technology advanced, the variety of data naturally increased. In the 1980s, with the technology of the time, data was fairly uniform and simple. But as the years went on, data became more diverse, and naturally, the way we stored it had to evolve as well. This is where the well-known 3VS problem emerged. Volume, velocity, and variety. Volume, the amount of data, exploded. Social media, sensors, IoT. Velocity, the speed of data flow increased dramatically. Real-time data, logs, streaming. Variety, the diversity of data grew. Tables, JSON, images, videos, plus logs. Since data warehouses could only handle structured, row-and-column-based data, they could not cope with the complexity brought by the 3VS. Billions of rows of massive data, real-time streaming logs, and semi-structured or unstructured formats such as JSON, images, and videos became a huge challenge for traditional systems. As a solution to these problems, in the 2000s, the concept of the data lake emerged. Data lakes could store all types of data, structured, semi-structured, and even unstructured, for example, JSON, log files, images, videos. And thanks to cloud storage solutions, they were cheap and highly scalable. For data scientists, this was extremely valuable because they could directly access raw data. But there was one problem. When analysts tried to prepare reports from this lake, they ran into performance issues. In other words, for BI, business intelligence, reporting, a data lake was not strong enough. Let's break this down. The data scientist's job, building machine learning models, making predictions, running experimental analyses. For them, the type and richness of data matters most. A data lake could store JSON, CSV, images, videos, log files, anything. The data scientist could process this data with Python or R and run their own algorithms. Performance was not as critical since model training could take a long time anyway and leverage CPU-slash-GPU power. The key was having access to diverse and accurate data. The data analyst's job, reports, dashboards, quick visualizations with BI tools. When a business analyst or manager asks in the morning meeting, show me the sales trend from the last three months, the analyst needs to produce a chart within minutes. But in a data lake, the data is raw and unorganized. Running a query can be slow because the data isn't stored in optimized formats. Meanwhile, in a data warehouse, the data was stored in optimized tables with indexes. Queries could run at light speed. But companies need to do both machine learning and business intelligence. That's why the lakehouse was born, to meet the needs of both sides within a single structure. The lakehouse became the common ground that unites the two. A lakehouse is a new architecture that combines the organization and performance of a data warehouse with the flexibility and cost-effectiveness of a data lake. This means you can store both structured and unstructured data in the same environment and from the same platform, perform both reporting and machine learning tasks. What the lakehouse provides. Thanks to the lakehouse architecture, companies no longer need to store their data in two separate places. In the past, data had to be copied from a data lake into a data warehouse, which meant both time loss and added cost. With the lakehouse, however, both business intelligence reporting and AI-slash-machine learning workloads are supported on a single platform. It also ensures data reliability through ACID transactions, meaning the data stays consistent. Yes, we will cover what ACID means in more detail later. In summary, today's data is not only large, but also diverse and fast-moving. The lakehouse addresses all three of these challenges at once. On a single platform, you can store raw data, perform structured data analysis, and provide a powerful environment for data scientists. Executives make decisions with confidence, and data scientists build models with trustworthy data. The lakehouse is the unified platform of modern data management. It combines the performance and structure of the data warehouse with the flexibility and cost advantage of the data lake. That concludes this lesson.
